Depth and Freshness, Not Learned Prediction: A Pre-Registered Study of
Prefetching from Disk Blocks to Product Pages

Ashvik Mishra (24BAI0342)
School of Computer Science and Engineering
Vellore Institute of Technology
Vellore, India

Shourya Saraf (24BDS0391)
School of Computer Science and Engineering
Vellore Institute of Technology
Vellore, India

Abstract—Kernels and browsers both prefetch. We ask which decision moves the
result, under rules fixed before the experiments ran. Disk (simulated): on 56
block traces from 4 corpora and a 1 MiB cache, a learned workload-class router
hit less often than plain sequential read-ahead (-0.94 pp [-1.31, -0.58]) with
1.078× the device I/Os of no prefetching, and a GPU-trained depth controller
less often than a validation-chosen fixed depth (-2.50 pp [-4.30, -0.80]),
while Linux-style window growth added +23.77 pp and cut device I/Os to 0.740×
of no prefetching's. Real devices (measured): on a hard disk and an SSD, the
mode with the highest simulated hit ratio was fastest in 0 of 15 cells, and
with exact cache parity prefetching raised the mean closed-loop latency on the
hard disk in 22 of 30 mode–trace cells. Web (simulated): on RetailRocket,
transition counts beat a trained ranker (+0.07 pp [+0.06, +0.09]), online
counts beat a GRU (-0.79 pp [-0.83, -0.76]), and index freshness gave
+1.52 pp [+1.49, +1.56]. Live HTTP (measured, emulated network): every
prefetching policy lowered the wait with a hard-disk store, by up to 8.27 ms
per view against 114.11 ms without prefetching, with exact simulator parity.
Better learners (simulated): none of 15 classifiers made the router beat
sequential read-ahead, a hindsight-trained router choosing depth did not beat
a per-trace fixed depth, and a tuned web ranker gained +0.15 pp in utility but
stayed below transition counts. How much to fetch and how fresh the statistics
are moved the results; no learned predictor beat its simple baseline, and
simulated hit ratio did not predict real-disk latency.

Index Terms—prefetching, read-ahead, page cache, storage traces, web
prefetching, cache simulation, machine learning for systems, pre-registration

I. Introduction
===============

Prefetching is a bet. The Linux page cache reads ahead of a sequential
reader [1], [2], storage arrays stage blocks [3], [4], and browsers or proxies
fetch pages a user may open next [5], [6]. Each spends device or network work
now to hide latency later, and must decide what to fetch, how much, and when
to stop. A growing literature trains predictors for the first
decision [7]–[11].

We ask whether the predictor is the decision that matters, in two domains with
one model: disk blocks under a page cache and product pages under a
per-session browser cache. Before each experiment ran, we committed its
comparisons, baselines, sample sizes, intervals and decision rules to the
repository. The hypothesis was that the learned predictor is the smallest
lever and depth, feedback and freshness the large ones. Every claim below is
the verdict of one of those rules, computed from the results files by a script
and listed in Table V in the appendix.

The hypothesis held only in part. No learned predictor we tried beat its
simple, fair baseline: the class router lost to sequential read-ahead, the
GPU-trained depth controller to a validation-chosen fixed depth, the logistic
ranker to transition counts, and the GRU sequence model to online counts. A
follow-up study asked whether a better learner would change that; it did not,
and its one gain, a tuned ranker's for the web controller, was small next to
freshness. How much to prefetch, and how fresh the statistics were, moved the
simulated hit ratio by far more. Yet neither study's ordering rule calls the
predictor the smallest lever. On disk, the feedback gate, which we expected to
matter, moved the hit ratio as little as the predictor did, or less; on the
web, the sequence model's loss could not be separated from the depth lever's
gain. And on a real hard disk, a higher simulated hit ratio did not mean a
lower measured latency.

Our contributions are:

  - A pre-registered, multi-corpus disk study (56 traces, 4 corpora, paired
    and corpus-cluster bootstrap intervals) in which a learned class router
    and a learned depth controller both lose to simple baselines, while depth
    and Linux-style window growth carry the gains; window growth is the only
    family that also reduces device I/Os.
  - A real Linux testbed that replays the simulator's exact plan on a hard
    disk and an SSD, with the cache behaviour asserted equal on every request
    (150 O_DIRECT runs), which shows simulated hit ratio failing to rank
    measured latency.
  - A web decomposition on RetailRocket that separates the ranker, index
    freshness, depth, the controller and a feedback gate, each with a paired
    session-bootstrap interval.
  - A live HTTP replay of the validated web policies over loopback with an
    emulated network, with 0 mismatches between the live and simulated cache
    counters.
  - A tuned comparison of 15 classifier families for the disk router, a
    hindsight-labelled router that can choose read-ahead depth, and tuned web
    rankers, which locates the limit in the router's action space and in index
    freshness rather than in the choice of learner.

the systems and Section V the protocol. Sections VI–IX report the four

II. Background and Related Work
===============================

Read-ahead in the kernel. Linux keeps a per-file read-ahead window that grows
while the reader stays sequential and collapses on a random access; a marker
page triggers the next asynchronous batch [1], [2], [12], and applications can
add hints [13], [14]. Our linux_ra emulates this window; the testbed measures
the kernel's own.

Prefetching in storage caches. Sequential prefetching with adaptive degree and
trigger distance is standard in storage controllers [3], [4]; other work
exploits disk layout and access history [15], mined block correlations [16] or
server-side context [17]. Informed prefetching uses application hints and
bounds how far ahead to fetch [18], and integrated prefetching and caching
studies when a prefetch should replace a cached block [19]. Predictive file
prefetching goes back to probability graphs and Markov predictors [20]–[22].

Learned prefetching. LSTM models of address deltas [8], [23] have been applied
to SSD traces [7] and to flash (a non-archival workshop abstract [24]). Online
and streaming learners adapt file-system prefetching as the workload
changes [10], [25]. Baleen learns admission and prefetch for flash caches [9],
CAPSULE clusters spatio-temporal locality for cloud block storage [26], KML
runs models inside the kernel [27], and CrossPrefetch coordinates prefetching
across the OS and the library [28]. Memory-side work learns remote-memory
prefetch and page placement [29], [30]. Such work usually compares a learned
model with one heuristic on a few traces; we fix the simple baselines in
advance and measure the other levers on the same traces.

Reference points from cache theory. Belady's MIN is the optimal demand-paging
replacement [31]; it does not bound a policy that prefetches, and we never use
it as such a bound. Stack-distance analysis [32] is the classical evaluation
tool, and adaptive, FIFO-based and learned eviction [33]–[37] change the
replacement policy; we keep LRU fixed so that only prefetching varies.

Web prefetching and next-item models. Predictive prefetching of web pages [5],
its latency bounds [38] and its cost over slow links [6] raised the same what
and how-much questions in the 1990s. Session-based next-item models range from
GRUs to self-attention [11], [39]; we test a GRU as a ranker.

We do not propose a predictor; we measure, under pre-registered rules, which
lever moves the outcome, and check the simulators against a real disk and real
HTTP.

III. A Common Model of Prefetching
==================================

Both domains share one abstraction (Fig. 5 in the appendix): a demand stream
(the 512-byte blocks of each disk request, or the views of each web session)
reads through a bounded LRU cache, and a policy issues speculative fetches
into it. The policy decides which items to fetch (the predictor), how many (a
depth or a growing window) and whether to fetch at all (a gate that watches
its own precision).

Metrics. The hit ratio counts hits per read block on disk and per view on the
web. Precision is useful over issued prefetches; the wasted share is its
complement. On disk we also count device I/Os (operations after merging
contiguous blocks) and blocks moved, relative to no prefetching, because a
higher hit ratio can cost more device work.

Utility. With c the cost of one speculative fetch in units of one hit,

    U(c) = (hits - c · issued)/requests .                                  (1)

A policy is worth running at cost c when its U(c) exceeds that of no
prefetching; the c at which the two are equal is the policy's break-even c*.
The disk study's latency model charges h, m and p microseconds for a hit, a
demand miss and a prefetch per block. Its mean cost per block rearranges to
m - (m-h) U(c) with c = p/(m-h), so ranking policies by modelled latency is
ranking them by U at that one c. With the assumed h = 5, m = 100 and p = 50 μs
this is c = 0.526; with a prefetch that costs as much as a miss and a hit at
one hundredth of a miss it is c = 1.0101: above one, so a policy would have to
gain more than one hit per prefetch to beat no prefetching, which no precision
allows. That threshold is a property of costing every block as one device
read, not of any hardware. The web c is an assumed number of hit-equivalents;
the live study measures waits instead.

Positioning cost. A real device charges positioning once per I/O and transfer
per block. With κ = t_pos/t_xfer, a policy's modelled device time falls below
no prefetching's when κ exceeds a per-trace threshold κ* derived from its
device-I/O and block counts. Section V gives the measured κ of our two
devices.

IV. Systems
===========

IV-A. Disk simulator and policies
---------------------------------

One causal replay loop serves every disk policy: a request first reads its
blocks through the LRU cache, and only then may the policy prefetch for later
requests; window-based policies never prefetch in their first window. The
policies are none; sequential (two blocks past each request); strided and
stride (a repeated address stride); markov (address-delta transitions);
adaptive, a Gaussian naive Bayes classifier that labels each window with one
of four workload classes and routes it to the matching policy;
adaptive_evidence, a rule-based router on the same features; three
feedback-gated modes (guard, depth_adaptive, correlate) that issue only while
their observed precision beats the cost model's break-even; deep@d, fixed
read-ahead of d blocks; and linux_ra@w, an emulation of the kernel's
read-ahead window with a ceiling of w blocks. Two modes are trained: an LSTM
over bucketed address deltas, and learned_depth, a network that every 32
requests predicts which read-ahead segments will be read soon and sets the
depth to the deepest likely one. The classifier's accuracy was measured only
on synthetic windows; the traces carry no workload labels, and we claim
nothing about its accuracy on them.

IV-B. Real Linux testbed
------------------------

The testbed executes the simulator's own plan on a real device: the simulator
runs once with a recording cache, and every hit, miss and prefetch becomes an
instruction. Backend B keeps a user-space LRU with the simulator's rules,
reads misses with O_DIRECT [40], prefetches on four threads, and asserts on
every request that its hits and misses equal the plan, so only timing differs.
Backend A reads through the kernel page cache in a memory-limited cgroup [41]
and prefetches with POSIX_FADV_WILLNEED [13], issued in chunks of at most
256 KiB because the kernel truncates longer hints; its hit ratio under the
multi-generational LRU [42] is a measured divergence, never forced to match.
Backend A also measures the kernel's own read-ahead (kernel_ra, kernel_seq)
and a gate run inline without a plan, and reads residency with cachestat and
RWF_NOWAIT reads [43], [44]. Before every run the trace's pages are dropped
and the drive cache is displaced; run order is shuffled.

IV-C. Web replay
----------------

Each session has an LRU browser cache of C objects that evicts unused
prefetched objects first. After each view a scorer ranks candidates: a
logistic ranker, transition counts from the index split (trans_s0), causal
online counts updated as the test split replays (trans_online), or a GRU [11].
A controller turns the ranking into fetches: fixedk takes the top k; adaptive
picks the depth in {0,1,2,4,8} that maximises the summed predicted probability
minus a per-fetch penalty (adaptive_ca after dropping resident items); gate
fetches two items above a confidence threshold; and feedback fetches only
where the observed precision of a (rank, score-bin) bucket exceeds c,
exploring with probability ε.

IV-D. Live HTTP replay
----------------------

An asyncio origin serves objects with a real pread from an object store on the
SSD or the hard disk and emulates the round trip and a per-session link that
serves demand before prefetch. Emulated browsers replay held-out sessions at
their recorded gaps with the simulator's cache class and record each view's
wait. A pass is valid only if its counters equal the simulator's in every
session, timer lags stay in bounds and the host is quiet.

Disk traces and configurations. We use the 56 oracleGeneral block traces of
four corpora (Table I): MSR Cambridge [45], FIU [46], Alibaba [47] and
Tencent [48], [49], as redistributed with libCacheSim [50]–[52]. A record
holds a whole-second timestamp, a byte offset and a size, with no read/write
flag or service time, so writes replay as reads. FIU stores one record per
4 KiB page, which we merge back into requests (same second, contiguous, at
most 1024 sectors); the other corpora's records are requests. 15 traces form
near-duplicate families. The headline configuration C1 replays the first
1,000,000 canonical requests of every trace through 1 MiB and 64 MiB caches,
every policy under the assumed cost model and the gated modes also at the
break-even model. C2 sweeps depth on the first 250,000 requests, C3 replays a
second slice (after 1,000,000 requests) of 21 subset traces, and C4 repeats
FIU at a merge cap of 256 sectors. The trained modes are scored only in the
GPU study: on each subset trace's first 2,000,000 requests, the first 60 %
trains, the next 20 % selects thresholds and the validation-chosen fixed depth
(VCF), and only the last 20 % is scored, over 5 seeds.

Real device. The testbed replays the first 20,000 requests of 5 traces through
a 32 MiB cache (Backend A's cgroup cannot hold 1 MiB), 3 repeats per cell, in
a closed loop and, for msr_hm_0, with each recorded second's requests spread
over that second. The headline device is an idle hard disk (ST10000NM017B-2T,
t_pos = 5665.4 μs, κ = 2957 blocks); the root SSD (Micron_5300_MTFD, κ = 112)
is shared with other tenants and runs a reduced grid, and a cell with over one
percent foreign I/O is not used.

Web and live HTTP. RetailRocket [53] gives 2,664,218 views in 1,755,863
sessions (split by a 30-minute gap). Four chronological splits build the
candidate index (1,065,687 views), train the ranker (532,844), validate
(532,843) and test (532,844). Every choice, each controller's parameter
included, is made on validation separately per capacity (2, 4, 8, 16, 32
objects) and cost (c in 0, 0.01, 0.05, 0.25); the test split is replayed once.
The live study replays 2,000 test sessions (6,685 views) at capacity 8 with an
emulated 50 ms round trip, a 10 Mbit/s link and lognormal object sizes of
median 64 KiB, all ASSUMED; each store runs 3 repetitions of no prefetching,
two fixed depths and the three controllers E4 validated at c = 0.01.

Classifier study. The three experiments of Section X were pre-registered after
E1–E6 had been run. Each tunes only on training or validation data (synthetic
training seeds, the first disk slice with corpus-grouped folds, web split 2),
scores its test data once, and uses the bootstraps and verdict words of the
study it extends.

Statistics. Disk intervals are 95 % percentile intervals of a paired bootstrap
over traces and of a two-level cluster bootstrap over corpora and traces with
twin families down-weighted (10,000 resamples, seed 42). A disk policy is
better or worse only when both agree; a paired-only result is reported as not
robust to clustering. Web and live intervals are paired session bootstraps,
real-device ones Student-t over paired repeats. All comparisons and rules were
committed before the experiments; the two amendments (smaller real-device and
live grids) were committed before their first run. The host (128 CPUs, kernel
6.14.0-37-generic) and its NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell Workstation Edition
GPU were shared, so GPU times are contended and never quoted as costs. The
models use PyTorch [54] and scikit-learn [55].

How much to prefetch. This is the largest lever (P3, P4). Fixed read-ahead
(deep@8) is better than no prefetching at both capacities (+6.17 pp [+4.28,
+8.10] paired and +5.79 pp [+2.98, +8.41] cluster at 1 MiB, winning on 52 of
56 traces). In the C2 depth sweep the deepest depth tested (512 blocks) has
the highest mean hit ratio in 14 of the 16 corpus–cache curves; only at 1 MiB
do Alibaba and MSR peak earlier, at 128 and 256 blocks (Fig. 6 in the
appendix). In C1, deep@256 adds +35.56 pp at 1 MiB and +49.48 pp at 64 MiB.
Linux-style window growth gets much of that gain with far less waste:
linux_ra@256 adds +23.77 pp [+19.47, +28.35] at 1 MiB, wins on all 56 traces,
and leaves 23.02 % of its prefetches unused, against 80.72 % for deep@256.
Belady's MIN for demand paging reaches 22.79 % at 1 MiB (none: 14.85 %);
several prefetching policies exceed it, as they may: MIN bounds replacement,
not prefetching.

Hit ratio is not device work (P5, P6; Fig. 1). At 1 MiB, deep@8 increases
device I/Os (1.066× [1.030×, 1.118×] of none's), and deep@256 moves 7.015× as
many blocks. The only policies that reduce device I/Os at either capacity are
linux_ra@64, linux_ra@256, linux_ra@1024: window growth merges its reads into
larger requests, so linux_ra@256 needs 0.740× [0.687×, 0.791×] of none's I/Os
at 1 MiB while moving 1.086× the blocks; every prefetching policy moves more
blocks than none. Whether that pays depends on the device: with the measured
hard-disk κ, linux_ra@256 lowers modelled device time on 54 of 56 traces and
deep@8 on 8 (Fig. 7 in the appendix).

The learned class router. The classifier-routed adaptive policy is worse than
sequential at both capacities (P1): -0.94 pp [-1.31, -0.58] paired and
-0.81 pp [-1.25, -0.33] cluster at 1 MiB, losing on 49 of 56 traces, and
-1.21 pp [-1.52, -0.92] at 64 MiB (losing on 54). It still beats none
(+0.92 pp [+0.37, +1.85]), but by less than the two-block read-ahead it routes
between, and with more device I/Os (1.078×).

The feedback gate. Against sequential, guard is worse at 64 MiB
(-0.22 pp [-0.30, -0.16]) and at 1 MiB its result is “paired interval only;
not robust to corpus clustering” (paired -0.46 pp [-0.73, -0.14], cluster
-0.44 pp [-0.80, +0.02]). At the break-even cost model it is worse at both
capacities and stays within +0.20 pp of none. The gate limits waste and device
work (1.026× of none's I/Os, against 1.055× for sequential) but adds no hits
over sequential. The gated mode that does add hits is depth_adaptive, which
lets the same precision evidence also choose a read-ahead depth: at the
assumed cost model it is above sequential by +1.44 pp [+0.46, +2.55] paired
(+1.01 pp [+0.08, +2.17] cluster) at 1 MiB and +3.05 pp [+2.02, +4.18] at
64 MiB (exploratory: not a pre-registered comparison), and at the break-even
model it is identical to guard. Its gain comes with a choice of depth, not
with the gate alone.

Which lever is smallest. The pre-registered ordering compares the intervals of
|Δ| against sequential. At 1 MiB the predictor ([0.58, 1.31] pp paired) and
the feedback gate ([0.14, 0.73]) cannot be ordered; both are smaller than
depth ([3.05, 5.62]) and window growth ([17.75, 26.38]), and depth is smaller
than window growth, in both the paired and the cluster intervals. At 64 MiB
the order is complete: feedback, then the predictor, then depth, then window
growth. So the rule's verdict at both capacities is that the predictor is not
the smallest lever: the learned predictor moves the hit ratio little, but the
feedback gate moves it as little or less.

Robustness. Of the 7 P1–P4 decisions that C3 repeats, 6 are robust on a second
slice of the subset traces; deep@32 against none is prefix-dependent (better
on the first slice, paired-only on the second). On FIU, 7 of 7 are
cap-insensitive between merge caps of 256 and 1024 sectors. Request
reconstruction matters a great deal: on FIU at C2, deep@8 changes the hit
ratio by +2.06 pp [-4.19, +6.32] on merged requests (an interval that contains
zero) but by +60.18 pp [+41.63, +75.76] on the unmerged 4 KiB page records, an
artefact of counting each page as a request that we report only as a
sensitivity view.

Learned depth and the LSTM. The GPU-trained depth controller is worse than the
validation-chosen fixed depth at both action caps, on held-out test segments:
-2.38 pp [-3.67, -1.14] with a 64-block cap (cluster -2.38 pp [-3.67, -1.01];
it wins on 3 of 21 traces) and -2.50 pp [-4.30, -0.80] with a 256-block cap,
over 5 seeds. The GPU study records prefetches, not device I/Os: the
controller issues 0.84× and 1.23× as many as VCF under the two caps. It does
beat deep@8 (exploratory; +24.08 pp [+15.18, +33.85]), but deep@8 is not the
fair baseline: the depth a validation split chooses is. The LSTM predicts
address deltas, so we compare only its cache outcome: on its 4 traces its mean
test hit ratio (24.26 %) is below linux_ra's (33.41 %) on 4 of 4 and close to
none's (24.50 %); its contended GPU timings are not quoted.

VIII. Web Results (Simulated)
=============================

All numbers here replay the RetailRocket test split (364,364 sessions) through
per-session caches with instant prefetch, with an assumed c. Table VI in the
appendix isolates one lever per row against its pre-registered baseline.

The controller. Selected on validation separately at each capacity, the
adaptive controller (adaptive_0.01) beats the best fixed depth at the same
capacity (W1; +0.36 pp [+0.33, +0.38] in U at c = 0.01 and capacity 8,
+0.58 pp [+0.55, +0.61] at capacity 32), beats no prefetching (W2;
+0.44 pp [+0.41, +0.48]), and, in an exploratory comparison, beats transition
top-1 (+0.30 pp [+0.27, +0.32]). By decision rule 1 we may therefore say that
the adaptive controller beats fixed prefetching at capacities 8 and 32, at
this cost, and no more. It gets there by issuing less: 0.73 prefetches per
view against 0.84 for the best fixed depth, with a hit ratio of 12.63 %
against 12.38 % (11.45 % without prefetching; every capacity and family is in
Table XII).

The ranker and its freshness. The trained logistic ranker loses to plain
history: top-1 transition counts from the index split hit more often at depth
one (W3; +0.07 pp [+0.06, +0.09]), although, as an exploratory diagnostic, the
logistic ranker is the better-calibrated of the two (Brier skill +0.013
against -4.493 for the counts). Counts updated online as the test split
replays are worth far more (W4, online counts minus the logistic ranker at
depth four; +1.52 pp [+1.49, +1.56]). Descriptively, the next item is in the
online index's top eight for 17.53 % of eligible views, against 9.08 % for the
shipped index (Fig. 8 in the appendix).

Depth. Prefetching four items instead of one raises the hit ratio (W5;
+0.80 pp [+0.77, +0.83]) but costs more than it gains at c = 0.01
(-1.84 pp [-1.87, -1.81] in U): only 0.50 % of its prefetches are used,
against 1.13 % at depth one. Depth is a lever in both directions, which is
consistent with a controller that chooses it per view beating the validated
best fixed depth.

The feedback gate. Against the adaptive controller, the validated gate
(feedback_0.01_eps0.005) gets the verdict “A worse” in hit ratio (W6;
-0.11 pp [-0.13, -0.10]) and “no detectable difference” in U (-0.01 pp [-0.02,
+0.01]), so by rule 3 it is not described as improving on the adaptive
controller. It does issue fewer prefetches (0.63 per view against 0.73).

The sequence model. The GRU, trained on the index and training splits over 5
seeds, reaches a hit ratio of 13.90 % at depth four (seed range 13.89 % to
13.91 %), below the online transition counts' 14.69 % on every seed; the
pre-registered comparison (W8, seed 0) gives “A worse” (-0.79 pp [-0.83,
-0.76]).

Scope and the lever rule. One cache per visitor instead of per session would
add +6.73 pp [+6.64, +6.82] of hits with no prefetching and an unbounded cache
(W7); this is an upper bound on cross-session reuse, not a prefetching result.
By rule 2, the ranker lever (|Δ| in [0.06, 0.09] pp) is smaller than both
depth ([0.77, 0.83]) and freshness ([1.49, 1.56]), but the sequence-model
lever ([0.76, 0.83]) overlaps depth, so the predictor is not called the
smallest lever and we make no ordering claim between the sequence model and
depth.

X. Can a Better Learner Help? (Simulated)
=========================================

The disk router of Section VI uses Gaussian naive Bayes [56], [57] to put each
32-request window in one of four classes, and each class prefetches at most
two blocks, so even a perfect classifier can do little better than sequential.
We tested that bound three ways, under rules committed after E1–E6 were known
(so not blind to them): replace the classifier, give a router read-ahead depth
as an action, and replace the web ranker.

Methods. Besides the shipped online GNB and QDA we tuned 13 scikit-learn
families [55], from discriminant analysis and logistic regression to kNN,
SVMs, CART, forests, boosting and an MLP (the list with its citations is in
the appendix), each with 16 random-search candidates [58] chosen on training
seeds alone; test seeds were scored once. The fitted models then routed
adaptive unchanged on C3's second slice of 19 traces. On synthetic windows
(appendix, Table XV) the tree models and boosting match GNB under the
block-size shift that made us keep it, and beat it on heavy phase blends, an
optimistic synthetic margin.

[Figure 3: The action-space ceiling (simulated, C3 slice 2, 19 traces): mean
hit ratio against device I/Os relative to none. The 15 routed classifiers sit
with sequential; depth, window growth and the hindsight routers move away from
it. VCF is the validation-chosen fixed action. — see
paper/figures/f9_action_space.pdf]

Routing real traces (Fig. 3, Table XVI). By the claim rule, no routed
classifier beats read-ahead at either capacity: all 15 show no difference
detected against sequential at 1 MiB and all are worse at 64 MiB (the best, an
MLP, by -0.34 pp [-0.51, -0.20]). Against the shipped GNB, only the random
forest is better at 1 MiB (+0.25 pp [+0.07, +0.55] paired, +0.25 pp [+0.05,
+0.85] cluster), and 8 of 14 alternatives are at 64 MiB. Every routed
classifier increases device I/Os (1.027×–1.160× of none's at 1 MiB), while
linux_ra@256 adds +21.04 pp over sequential with 0.713× of the I/Os. The
classifiers agree with GNB on only 0.29–0.70 of windows, yet the hit ratio
barely moves: the class decides little when every class prefetches two blocks
at most.

Depth as an action. The hindsight router picks the next window's action among
none, sequential, deep@8, deep@32 and deep@256. Its label is the action that
did best on that window in its own fixed replay, a trajectory approximation in
the spirit of hindsight-labelled caching [59] and open to the distribution
shift of imitation learning [60]. We selected among 8 families by
corpus-grouped cross-validation on the first slice and scored the second
(Table XVII). Against the per-trace validation-chosen fixed action (VCF), the
router shows no difference detected at 1 MiB (+0.027 pp [-1.995, +2.259]) and
is worse, by a negligible -0.003 pp, at 64 MiB, where it sends 99.9 % of
windows to deep@256, the VCF action. Its gains over sequential (+29.809 pp at
1 MiB) and linux_ra@256 are depth's, at 3.649× the blocks moved by none. A
device-objective router reduces device I/Os (0.976× of none's), but
linux_ra@256 needs only 0.713×. GNB had the lowest cross-validation score in 6
of 6 cells, yet the best family does not beat VCF; nor is the trajectory
oracle a true ceiling, since it beats VCF at 1 MiB only.

Web rankers (Table XVIII). On the same candidates and features, tuned
boosting, forests, extra trees and an MLP rank better than the shipped
logistic ranker (MLP: +0.23 pp [+0.20, +0.27] in MRR@8). By the primary rule,
a tuned alternative ranker improves the controller at both capacities, the one
learned predictor here that does: the validation-selected MLP raises U(c) per
view at c = 0.01 by +0.15 pp [+0.13, +0.18] at capacity 8 and +0.15 pp [+0.12,
+0.17] at 32, though its hit ratio at capacity 8 is lower (-0.16 pp [-0.18,
-0.14]). No family beats transition top-1 at depth 1, and all stay far below
online counts (MLP: -1.41 pp [-1.45, -1.37]): a better ranker does not close
the freshness gap.

XI. Cross-Domain Discussion
===========================

[Figure 4: Simulated utility U(c) of every policy (grey) and its upper
envelope (coloured by the winning policy) against the cost of one prefetch.
Left: disk, C1, 1 MiB, means over 56 paired traces, with the two cost models
marked. Right: web test split, logistic ranker, capacity 8, with the assumed
costs marked. The envelope is computed in hindsight on the test data and is
not a selection. — see paper/figures/f8_utility_envelopes.pdf]

One objective, two domains. Fig. 4 puts both domains on the axis of Eq. (1).
On disk, the deepest read-ahead (deep@256) is best when a prefetch is free,
window growth (linux_ra@256) at the assumed cost model's c, and no prefetching
at the break-even model's c above one. On the web, the best policy at every
marked positive cost is an adaptive controller that chooses its depth per view
(adaptive_ca_0.01 at c = 0.01, adaptive_0.2 at the largest marked cost). In
both domains the policy that wins changes with the price of a prefetch, and it
is a choice of how much to fetch.

The hypothesis, by its rules. The pre-registered hypothesis had three parts.
The predictor is the smallest lever: not established in either study. No
learned predictor beat its simple, fair baseline (the class router lost to
sequential, the depth network to the validation-chosen depth, the logistic
ranker to transition counts, the GRU to online counts), but on disk the
feedback gate moved the hit ratio as little or less, and on the web the GRU's
loss could not be separated from the depth lever's gain. Depth and freshness
are large levers: supported by the orderings. On disk, depth and window growth
are larger than both the predictor and the gate at both cache sizes; on the
web, freshness is larger than the ranker. Feedback is a large lever: not
supported. The disk gate is no better than sequential and the web gate shows
no detectable difference from the adaptive controller; both mainly issue less.
The one gated disk mode above sequential, depth_adaptive, also chooses its
depth (an exploratory comparison).

Is the learner the problem? No, in either domain. On disk, any of 15
classifiers leaves the router level with or below sequential, because its
actions prefetch two blocks at most; with depth as an action a router only
reaches a fixed depth chosen per trace, moving 3.649× the blocks of no
prefetching at 1 MiB. On the web a better ranker helps the controller
slightly, but transition top-1 and online counts stay ahead. A learner matters
only once its decision can move the result, and even then depth and freshness
decide more.

Simulation against measurement. The two real-system studies point in opposite
directions, and both are measured. On a hard disk with O_DIRECT and a closed
loop, the simulator's higher hit ratio turned into higher latency in most
cells, plausibly because prefetch reads compete with demand reads for one
device. Over HTTP, the link serves demand first, a prefetched object skips
both the round trip and the disk, and the instant-prefetch model understated
the saving on the hard-disk store. The simulator's hit ratio is therefore a
statement about the cache, not about latency, and which way the error goes
depends on where the bottleneck sits.

For a system designer. Choose how much to prefetch per workload and per price
of a prefetch, prefer window growth that merges reads when device operations
are the cost, keep the statistics fresh, and validate on the real device
before trusting a simulated hit ratio. A learned predictor must beat these
baselines on held-out data to earn its training cost; none of ours did, tuned
or not.

XII. Threats to Validity
========================

Trace format. The oracleGeneral records carry no read/write flag and no
service time, and their timestamps are whole seconds; writes are replayed as
reads, and think time on the real device is reconstructed from those seconds.
The FIU page-merge rule is our reconstruction; the sensitivity rows show that
leaving pages unmerged would change the FIU depth gain drastically. The disk
study replays a prefix of each trace (C3 checks a second slice), and
near-duplicate traces are down-weighted only in the cluster bootstrap.

Model assumptions. Both simulators assume a prefetch completes instantly; the
real-device and live studies measure what that assumption hides, but only on 5
trace prefixes and one web sample. The simulators use LRU while the kernel
uses its multi-generational LRU, which is why Backend A's hit ratio is
reported as a divergence. Web object sizes, the round-trip time and the
bandwidth are assumed, the cache is per session, sessions are cut by a fixed
gap, and all web data come from one retailer.

Statistics. No correction for multiple comparisons is applied; we state only
the pre-registered comparisons as findings and label the rest exploratory. The
real-device intervals rest on 3 repeats and are wide. The learned-depth
comparison had no pre-registration of its own and is read with the disk rule.
Trained artifacts are scored only on data they were not trained or selected
on.

The classifier study was pre-registered after E1–E6 were known. Its synthetic
accuracies favour the phase blends (a window's context comes from earlier
windows of its class) and are never real-trace accuracies; the hindsight
labels approximate the counterfactual by each action's own trajectory, and its
search was cut by a dated amendment; prediction times were contended; the MSRC
check uses seven size-selected files whose flag tracks address contiguity.

Environment. The host and GPU were shared; load and foreign I/O were recorded
for every run, contended cells and flagged passes are excluded, and no GPU
time is quoted as a cost. Each real-device result holds for its trace, think
model and device, not for hard disks or SSDs in general.

XIII. Conclusion and Future Work
================================

Across 56 block traces and the RetailRocket sessions, under rules fixed before
the experiments ran, the decisions that moved prefetching were how much to
fetch and how fresh the statistics were. Window growth gained +23.77 pp of
simulated hit ratio while reducing device I/Os, and online counts gained
+1.52 pp [+1.49, +1.56] on the web. No learned predictor we trained beat its
simple baseline, and the pre-registered feedback gates limited waste without
adding hits; the one gated mode that did add hits also chose depth. Tuning or
replacing the classifier did not change this: the router's actions, not its
learner, cap its gain, and a router given depth as an action only matched a
fixed depth chosen per trace. On a real hard disk the simulated gains did not
become lower latency in most O_DIRECT cells, while over emulated HTTP with a
hard-disk store the measured savings exceeded the model's. Future work should
test learned depth and admission against window growth on the real device
rather than in simulation, measure the web cost c instead of assuming it, and
repeat the live study on a real network.
