# Presentation outline, speaker notes and viva preparation

This is the talk for the final review: 15 slides covering both studies, each
with the figure it shows and what to say, followed by the questions an
examiner is most likely to ask and honest answers. The Beamer deck that
implements it is `presentation/slides.tex` (compile on Overleaf; see
`presentation/README.md`). Every figure is a file in `paper/figures/`,
generated by `python reproduce.py paper` from `results/`; every number on a
slide is a `paper/numbers.tex` macro, and every number below is read from
the `results/` file named next to it.

Time budget: about 15 minutes of talk, 1 minute per slide on average, with
the disk results (slides 6-9) and the real-system slides (10-11, 14) taking
the most. Split: Ashvik presents slides 1-9 (motivation, model, disk),
Shourya presents 10-15 (real device, web, live, conclusion); both take
questions. Rehearse the "what we found" sentence of each slide until it
comes out without notes.

The previous Review 2 guide (disk study only, MSRC-trace-003 numbers) was
removed from this file; it is in git history up to commit `787d0ad`.

## Slides

### 1. Title: What makes prefetching pay?

**Figure:** `paper/figures/f1_schematic.pdf` (the common cache/prefetch model).

**On the slide:** title, both names with roll numbers, School of Computer
Science and Engineering, VIT Vellore; one line: "two prefetching problems an
OS meets, simulated *and* measured".

**Say:** Prefetching fetches data before it is asked for. The kernel does it
for files (read-ahead), browsers and proxies do it for web pages. Machine
learning is often proposed to predict what to fetch. We tested, on public
data and on real hardware, whether the prediction is what matters.

### 2. The question and the pre-registered test

**Figure:** `paper/figures/f1_schematic.pdf`.

**On the slide:** the four levers: (i) *how much* to prefetch (depth or a
growing window), (ii) *feedback* on observed precision, (iii) *freshness* of
the statistics (web), (iv) the *learned predictor*. The hypothesis: the
predictor is the smallest lever.

**Say:** Before any experiment ran we wrote the comparisons and decision
rules into `results/*/PREREGISTERED.md`: what counts as "better", "worse"
or "no difference detected", and when one lever may be called smaller than
another. Every claim in this talk follows those rules, including the ones
that went against our hypothesis.

### 3. One model for both studies

**Figure:** `paper/figures/f8_utility_envelopes.pdf` (preview; explained on slide 15).

**On the slide:** a cache of fixed capacity, a demand stream, a policy that
issues speculative fetches. Metrics: hit ratio, precision, issued. The
objective `U(c) = (hits - c * issued) / requests`, with `c` the cost of one
prefetch in hits.

**Say:** The disk cost model is exactly `U` at one particular `c`, and the
web "cost per prefetch" is `U` at an assumed `c`, so one formula ranks
policies in both studies. The classic disk cost model puts that `c` above 1:
a prefetch would have to gain more than one hit per block. That is an
artefact of charging every 512-byte block as a full device read, which is
why we also count real device work (slide 7). Definitions:
`docs/METRICS.md`.

### 4. Disk data: 56 real block traces, reconstructed carefully

**Figure:** `paper/figures/f2_hit_vs_depth.pdf` (shows the corpora side by
side; the full datasets table is `paper/tables/t1_datasets.tex`).

**On the slide:** MSR Cambridge 14, FIU 10, Alibaba 20, Tencent 12 traces
(libCacheSim `oracleGeneral`); 24-byte records; byte offsets; FIU pages
merged back into requests; no read/write flag, no service times.

**Say:** The format was mis-described in our own early notes as one record
per block. It is one record per request (per 4 KiB page for FIU), with the
offset in bytes and, for two corpora, a volume tag in the high bits. Getting
this wrong changes hit ratios a lot, so the loader detects units per file
and every result names its reconstruction rule (`docs/DECISIONS.md` D20).

### 5. The disk simulator and its 14 policies

**Figure:** `paper/figures/f1_schematic.pdf`.

**On the slide:** the causal loop (demand, propose, prefetch; the first
window never prefetches); the families: fixed (`none`, `sequential`,
`deep@N`), per-request predictors (`stride`, `markov`, `lstm`), the trained
classifier router (`adaptive`), the evidence router, the feedback gate
(`guard`, `depth_adaptive`, `correlate`), the Linux read-ahead policy
(`linux_ra`) and the learned depth model (`learned_depth`).

**Say:** Every policy sees the same requests, cache and warm-up. A decision
at request *i* sees nothing after *i*; for the Linux read-ahead policy and
the learned depth model the tests check this directly, by replacing the
future with random requests and getting identical prefix outcomes. The
streamed replay equals the in-memory one bit for bit. `linux_ra` emulates
the kernel's on-demand read-ahead policy (sync window on a sequential miss,
async ramp when the marker block is hit).

### 6. Disk result 1: how much to prefetch dominates

**Figure:** `paper/figures/f2_hit_vs_depth.pdf`.

<!-- source: results/disk/table_main.csv config=C1 merge=auto latency=assumed corpus=all capacity=2048 -->
**On the slide (1 MiB cache, 56 traces):** `none` 14.85%; `sequential`
16.71%; `deep@8` 21.03%; `deep@256` 50.41%; `linux_ra@256` 38.62%;
`belady_min` 22.79%.
<!-- source: none -->

**Say:** Hit ratio climbs with read-ahead depth well past the depth 8 the
original project chose, and it does not saturate with cache size either
(D20 corrects both earlier claims). `belady_min` is Belady's optimal
*demand* replacement with no prefetching: a reference for replacement, not
an upper bound on prefetching, and deep read-ahead exceeds it.

### 7. Disk result 2: hit ratio is not device work

**Figure:** `paper/figures/f3_device_io_vs_hit.pdf`.

<!-- source: results/disk/deltas.csv config=C1 corpus=all capacity=2048 latency=assumed baseline=none -->
**On the slide:** device I/Os against `none`: `deep@8` 1.066x [1.030,
1.118], `linux_ra@256` 0.740x [0.687, 0.791]; blocks moved: `deep@8`
1.169x, `deep@256` 7.015x.
<!-- source: none -->

**Say:** A block hit ratio counts blocks; a disk pays per I/O and per block
moved. Fixed-depth read-ahead raises the hit ratio while issuing *more* I/Os
than doing nothing, because each short read-ahead is its own I/O. Only the
Linux-style growing window raises hit ratio *and* lowers device I/Os. So
every hit ratio in our paper is shown next to the device-I/O and
blocks-moved ratios.

### 8. Disk result 3: the learned models

**Figure:** `paper/figures/f3_device_io_vs_hit.pdf` (the learned-depth table
is `paper/tables/t2c_learned_depth.tex`).

<!-- source: results/disk/deltas.csv config=C1 corpus=all latency=assumed metric=hit_ratio baseline=sequential -->
**On the slide:** `adaptive` minus `sequential`: -0.94 pp [-1.31, -0.58] at
1 MiB, -1.21 pp [-1.52, -0.92] at 64 MiB (worse at both; cluster intervals
agree). `guard` minus `sequential`: -0.46 pp at 1 MiB, -0.22 pp at 64 MiB.
<!-- source: none -->
`learned_depth` against a validation-chosen fixed depth: worse on most of
21 traces, 5 seeds (`results/gpu/disk_learned_depth.csv`).

**Say:** The trained classifier router loses to plain two-block read-ahead.
We then trained what the depth result suggested, a GPU model that predicts
*how far* to read ahead, and compared it with the strongest fair baseline, a
fixed depth chosen on validation. It lost too. The LSTM that predicts
address deltas is below the Linux read-ahead policy on all four traces it
was scored on, each on a held-out test segment (D19). By the pre-registered rule the predictor is smaller
than depth and window growth, but **not** the smallest lever: at 64 MiB the
feedback gate's effect is smaller still.

### 9. When does a policy pay on a real device? kappa*

**Figure:** `paper/figures/f4_kappa_cdf.pdf`.

<!-- source: results/realio/device_sdb.json; results/realio/device_ssd.json -->
**On the slide:** device time `T = N_io * t_pos + N_blocks * t_xfer`;
measured `kappa = t_pos / t_xfer`: HDD about 2957 blocks, SSD about 112.
<!-- source: none -->

**Say:** For each policy and trace, `kappa*` is the device ratio above which
it costs less device time than doing nothing. A policy that issues more I/Os
than `none` never pays, whatever the device. The vertical lines are the two
drives we measured. In C1 at 1 MiB (`results/disk/kappa.csv`) `kappa*` of
`linux_ra@256` is finite on 54 of 56 traces and every finite value lies left
of both device lines; `deep@8` is infinite on all but 8.

### 10. The real Linux testbed

**Figure:** `paper/figures/f5_sim_vs_measured.pdf` (layout; results next).

**On the slide:** the simulator decides, the testbed does the I/O.
Backend B: user-space LRU, `O_DIRECT`, asynchronous prefetch threads,
hit/miss **asserted equal** to the simulator on every request. Backend A: the
kernel page cache in a memory cgroup, prefetch as chunked
`POSIX_FADV_WILLNEED`; `kernel_ra` and `kernel_seq` measure Linux's own
read-ahead.

**Say:** Kernel facts we had to measure first: a WILLNEED hint is truncated
to 256 KiB, so we issue it in chunks; a page charged to another cgroup is not
re-charged, so we drop the whole file before every run; `cachestat` plus a
non-blocking read tell a ready hit from a late one. Every run is a fresh
process with the drive cache displaced, three repeats in shuffled order,
started only on a quiet host. Parity held on every Backend B run.

### 11. Real-device result: the simulator's favourite was never the fastest

**Figure:** `paper/figures/f5_sim_vs_measured.pdf`.

<!-- source: results/realio/realio_summary.csv device=sdb backend=odirect think=closed trace=msr_hm_0 -->
**On the slide:** in none of 16 cells was the highest simulated hit ratio
the lowest measured latency; `none` was fastest in 6. Example, HDD,
msr_hm_0, Backend B: `deep@64` has the best simulated hit ratio (55.92%
against `none`'s 36.10%) and is 63.74 us *slower* per request [59.69,
67.80], on a 582.1 us baseline.
<!-- source: none -->

**Say:** A prefetch that is still in flight when it is needed, or that
queues in front of a demand read, costs time the simulator never charges.
Simulated hit ratio did not rank the policies by measured latency in any HDD
cell; simulated device time did so in some and against it in one. These are
statements about five trace prefixes on one HDD (and a shared SSD), not
about disks in general (D21).

### 12. Web study: RetailRocket, and the stale index

**Figure:** `paper/figures/f6_freshness.pdf`.

<!-- source: results/web/freshness.csv split=test -->
**On the slide:** per-session browser cache; four chronological splits
(index, ranker, validation, test). Test coverage@8 of the next view: split-0
index 9.08%, splits 0-2 13.99%, causal online index 17.53%.
<!-- source: none -->

**Say:** The original web branch built its candidate index once from the
oldest data and called its coverage a ceiling. Most of that ceiling was
staleness: an index that keeps counting transitions it has already seen,
causally, covers almost twice as many next views. We also select every policy
on validation at *each* cache size; the original selected at one size and
reused it, which hid losses to no prefetching at small caches (W1).

### 13. Web result: what moves the needle

**Figure:** `paper/figures/f6_freshness.pdf` (the decomposition table is
`paper/tables/t5_web_decomposition.tex`).

<!-- source: results/web/decomposition.csv metric=hit_ratio -->
**On the slide (hit ratio, cache 8):** freshness +1.52 pp; depth +0.80 pp;
ranker (counting vs the trained logistic ranker) +0.07 pp; GRU vs counting
-0.79 pp; feedback gate vs adaptive -0.11 pp.
<!-- source: none -->

**Say:** On the web the learned ranker moves hit ratio less than depth or
freshness, but a GPU sequence model trained over five seeds overlaps depth,
so by the pre-registered rule the predictor is not called the smallest lever;
that sequence model is worse than simply counting transitions. The validated adaptive controller
still beats the best fixed depth at caches 8 and 32 for an assumed cost per
prefetch (W1, W3).

### 14. Live HTTP replay: measured, with exact parity

**Figure:** `paper/figures/f7_live_saved_vs_bytes.pdf`.

<!-- source: results/live/live_metrics.csv experiment=headline store=hdd -->
**On the slide:** 42 passes, all valid, 0 sessions differing from the
simulator. HDD store: the online-index controller saved 8.13-8.55 ms per view
at 2.20x the bytes; fixed depth 4 saved 7.95-8.53 ms at 6.71x.
<!-- source: none -->

**Say:** Each emulated browser's cache *is* the simulator's cache class, so
its counters must match exactly, and they did. The network is emulated (RTT
50 ms, 10 Mbps) and object sizes are assumed, but every wait is measured.
With a FIFO link (SSD store, one repetition), depth 8's saving was no longer
detectable: its interval includes 0, because speculative bytes queued in
front of demand fetches. With demand priority it saved time (W4).

### 15. Across both domains, and what we claim

**Figure:** `paper/figures/f8_utility_envelopes.pdf`.

**On the slide:** the `U(c)` envelopes of both studies; three take-aways:
(1) how much to prefetch, and in what shape, matters more than any learned
predictor we tried; (2) a simulated hit ratio is not latency and not device
work; (3) on the web, freshness beats sophistication. Limits: writes replayed
as reads; one HDD; emulated network; assumed web costs.

**Say:** Read the envelope left to right: at low cost per prefetch deep
policies win, and as the cost rises the best policy issues less and less,
until doing nothing wins. Where a domain sits on that axis decides which
policy pays, which is why we measured it on real devices instead of
assuming it. Close with the honesty statement: every number in the paper is
generated from `results/`, every comparison was pre-registered, and the
negative results are reported as found.

## Viva questions and answers

**Q: Your title says prediction is not what matters. Did the data support
that everywhere?**
No, and we say so. On the web the learned ranker is smaller than depth and
freshness, but the sequence model overlaps depth, so the rule does not let us
call the predictor the smallest lever. On disk the predictor is smaller than depth and
window growth, but at 64 MiB the feedback gate's effect is smaller than the
predictor's, so "the predictor is the smallest lever" is not written for disk
(`docs/DECISIONS.md` D20).

**Q: Is your classifier's accuracy on real traces good?**
We do not know, and we never claim it. The real traces carry no workload
labels; the held-out accuracy is on independently generated synthetic
windows only. On real traces we report what the classifier does to the
cache, which is worse than plain read-ahead.

**Q: Why compare `learned_depth` with a validation-chosen fixed depth and not
with `deep@8`?**
Because that is the strongest fair baseline: the same action cap, chosen on
the same validation segment. Beating `deep@8` would only show that 8 is a bad
depth for that trace. Against the fair baseline the learned model lost.

**Q: Is `belady_min` an upper bound?**
Only for demand replacement without prefetching. Prefetching can exceed it,
and deep read-ahead does. We label it as a reference, never as a bound.

**Q: Why should we believe a hit ratio from a simulator?**
As a cache statistic, because the replay is causal, deterministic, and
checked by tests and by the real testbed (Backend B reproduced the simulated
hit/miss sequence on every request). As a latency, we do not: the real
device showed the simulator's best policy was never the fastest.

**Q: What does "break-even 1.0101" mean, and is the disk cost column
meaningful?**
It is the break-even precision of the cost model with a hit at 1% of a
miss and a prefetch costing a full miss (`LatencyModel(1, 100, 100)` in
`results/disk/PREREGISTERED.md`, the shape of the original measured model):
the precision a prefetcher would need to beat no prefetching when every
512-byte block is charged a full device read. Since precision cannot exceed
1, the column is degenerate at that setting. That is the cost model, not the
hardware; we replaced it with device I/O counts and a calibrated
positioning-plus-transfer model.

**Q: Your traces have no read/write flag. Does that matter?**
Writes are replayed as reads, so the absolute hit ratios are not those of a
read-only cache. That is why we never compare them numerically with the older
MSRC-trace-003 figures, and why every comparison is between policies on the
same replayed stream.

**Q: How do you know the FIU reconstruction is right?**
We do not know it exactly, and we list it as a threat to validity. The FIU
records are 4 KiB pages with whole-second timestamps, so the original request
boundaries are lost; we merge same-second contiguous pages into requests with
a 1024-sector cap. The loader itself was cross-checked on MSR, where the
oracle offsets match the committed MSR sample row for row; that validates the
format decoding, not the FIU merge. The merge rule is therefore reported as
a sensitivity: unmerged pages and uncapped merging (C2) and a 256-sector cap
(C4).

**Q: The host and GPU were shared. Are your timings valid?**
Simulated results do not depend on timing. The real-device and live runs wait
for a quiet host, record the load average and the foreign I/O on the device,
and flag rows above 1% foreign I/O (`results/realio/PREREGISTERED.md`); the
HDD headline rows have none. GPU
training times are reported with their contention samples and are never
quoted as a cost.

**Q: Why is the HDD the headline device and not the SSD?**
The SSD is the system disk and carries other users' reads; several of its
rows exceed the foreign-I/O threshold. The HDD was idle. SSD rows are shown
as secondary, with their flags.

**Q: Is the live HTTP saving a real page-load improvement?**
It is a measured wait over loopback with an emulated 50 ms RTT and 10 Mbps
link and object sizes we assumed, because the dataset records none. It shows
how the simulator's counts translate into waiting time under those
assumptions, with exact counter parity, not a field measurement.

**Q: Why did the GRU lose to counting transitions?**
We measured that it lost, not why. Two facts bear on it: a cache gains only
from predicting items the session has not already loaded, and the online
transition counts keep learning during the test period while the GRU is
frozen after training. That is why sequence models are compared on the
novel-item ranking columns and on cache outcomes, never on raw hit@1.

**Q: Could you have tuned the test results?**
Every choice was made on validation (web) or a validation segment (disk
models) and frozen; the test split was replayed once. The comparisons, their
baselines and the decision words were fixed in `PREREGISTERED.md` before the
runs, with any later change logged as an amendment.

**Q: What would you do next?**
Make the growing-window policy adaptive to the measured device ratio, model
prefetch completion and queueing in the simulator, and repeat the real-device
study on more drives and longer traces.

**Q: Where is a number on a slide from?**
From `paper/numbers.tex`, which `paper/numbers.py` writes from `results/`;
the slides contain no typed numbers, and `tools/check_doc_numbers.py`
enforces the same for the docs.
